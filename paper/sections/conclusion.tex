\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Putting Coherent Extrapolated Volition in a system prompt did not make four current language models more aligned
than a classic assistant prompt did. The CEV text changed behaviour in two opposite directions. Added to the classic
prompt, it made models' answers to AI-risk questions more aligned, mostly those about their own survival. It also lowered
their support for the agency of the people they were helping, which was not significant after correction for
HumanAgencyBench as a whole. In exploratory analyses, the CEV prompt's losses lay in deferring decisions and avoiding
value manipulation. Answers that reason explicitly about humanity's extrapolated volition scored lower on three
evaluations, on HumanAgencyBench partly because they arose on harder items, and a judge-blinding test found no penalty
for the mention itself. An instruction to serve what people would want under idealised reflection can act, for an assistant
with a present user, as an instruction to overrule them. Value texts from alignment theory should be adapted for
assistants and tested on several axes before deployment, and our harness makes such tests cheap to run.
