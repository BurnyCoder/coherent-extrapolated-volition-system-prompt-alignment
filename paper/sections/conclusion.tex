\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Putting Coherent Extrapolated Volition in a system prompt did not make four current language models significantly
more aligned than a classic assistant prompt did, though our intervals cannot rule out gains of up to
\RCevClassicCiHighMax{}, and it lowered scores on our modified HumaneBench. That drop rests on two of the four models.
Added to the classic prompt, the CEV text produced more aligned answers to AI-risk questions than an equally long
neutral text. Part of that gap is the neutral text lowering scores, and the gain over the classic prompt alone is not
significant after correction. Which kinds of AI-risk question gain depends on the comparison. The same addition gave
lower HumanAgencyBench scores, significant within its contrast but not after correction over all primary tests or
with any single model left out. In exploratory analyses that also do not
survive correction, the CEV prompt's losses lay in deferring decisions and avoiding value manipulation. Answers that
explicitly mention humanity's extrapolated volition scored lower on three evaluations; on HumanAgencyBench, part of
that gap was already present under the classic prompt. A judge-blinding test found no sign that the judge penalises the mention itself.
Our qualitative examples suggest that an instruction to serve what people would want under idealised reflection may
act, for an assistant with a present user, as licence to overrule them, at least without the essay's own caveats,
which our excerpt leaves out. This is a hypothesis for larger studies, not
a finding. Value texts from alignment theory should be adapted for assistants and tested on several axes before
deployment, and our harness makes such tests cheap to run.
