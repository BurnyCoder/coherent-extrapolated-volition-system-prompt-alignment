% Abstract: what we did, why it matters, how, the evidence, and the most surprising result.
\begin{abstract}
We test whether putting Coherent Extrapolated Volition (CEV), Yudkowsky's proposal that an AI should do what
humanity would want ``if we knew more, thought faster, were more the people we wished we were'', into a language
model's system prompt makes the model behave in a more aligned way than a classic assistant system prompt.
Alignment theory has proposed such targets for over two decades. System prompts are the cheapest way to hand one to a
deployed model, but whether doing so helps, and on which dimensions, has not been measured. We ran four models (GPT,
Claude, Qwen and Llama) under five system prompts: none, classic, CEV, classic+CEV, and a length-matched neutral
control. Each condition was scored on six public evaluations chosen to mirror what CEV is for: human wellbeing, human
agency, manipulation, corrigibility and power-seeking, instrumental convergence, and agentic blackmail. In total
there are \RNSamples{} samples, paired by item except in the single agentic scenario; five of the six evaluations
are graded by an external LLM judge. Relative to the classic prompt, the CEV prompt significantly improved no
evaluation and lowered HumaneBench (\RHbCevClassicDiff, Holm $p=\RHbCevClassicHolm$). This drop depends on two of the
four models, and on unscorable answers being alike across conditions. Adding the CEV text to the classic prompt,
compared with an equally long neutral text, made answers to AI-risk questions more aligned
(\RAirClassiccevLenmatchDiff, $p=\RAirClassiccevLenmatchHolmAll$ after correction over all primary tests). This holds
under every version of the answer parser. About half of the gap is the neutral text lowering scores relative to the
classic prompt alone, against which the CEV text's gain (\RAirClassiccevClassicDiff) is not significant after
correction. The same addition gave lower HumanAgencyBench scores (\RHabClassiccevLenmatchDiff), which is not
significant after correction. On five of six evaluations,
\RMentionShareMin--\RMentionShareMax{} of answers under the CEV prompts explicitly mention CEV. On
HumanAgencyBench these score \RHabMentionAligned{} against \RHabNoMentionAligned{} for the rest. Part of this gap was
already present under the classic prompt, on the same items (\RHabMentionClassicAligned{} against
\RHabNoMentionClassicAligned). A judge-blinding test finds no sign that the judge penalises the mention itself. In
exploratory analyses that do not survive correction, the CEV prompt's largest losses are in deferring important
decisions to the user and in avoiding value manipulation.
\end{abstract}
