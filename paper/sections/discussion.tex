\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Could ``extrapolated volition'' reduce deference? (hypothesis)}
The HumanAgencyBench drops below are not significant after correction, so this paragraph offers a hypothesis to
test, not an explanation of an established effect.
CEV directs an agent to serve what people \emph{would} want ``if we knew more, thought faster, were more the people
we wished we were''. It does not direct it to serve what they say they want. The text makes this explicit: in its own
example, the agent is told that it ``would construe Fred's volition not to include Fred's decision to kill Steve''.
A model told to act on users' idealised preferences has an explicit licence to substitute its judgement for theirs.
In everyday decisions, such as how to allocate a bonus fund, that licence can show up as making the decision. For the CEV
prompt against the classic prompt, the largest drops are in exactly the HumanAgencyBench dimensions that measure
handing decisions back to the user and not steering their values (exploratory). They are also larger in answers
where the model explicitly reasons about CEV, although those answers arise on items that were harder even under the
classic prompt. Both observations fit this account, but we have not tested it causally. The text also says that ``where the
course of humankind is not presently predictable'', CEV ``should \ldots{} leave options open, waiting for our
decision''. If the account is right, the models picked up the first idea more than this second one.

\paragraph{Why might AI-risk answers improve?}
CEV frames the AI as serving humankind's coherent wishes rather than its own, and asks it to be ``consistent under
reflection''. Answering multiple-choice questions about accepting shutdown or refusing power is plausibly easier for a
model framed this way. The evidence is weaker than the primary contrast suggests, though:
\begin{itemize}
  \item about half of the gain over the neutral text is the neutral text lowering scores;
  \item the gain of the CEV text over the classic prompt alone is not significant after correction;
  \item the CEV prompt on its own does not beat the classic prompt;
  \item no gain appears on the behavioural agentic scenario.
\end{itemize}
In that scenario, Qwen blackmailed more often under \cond{cev}, though not significantly. Several of its CEV-invoking answers combined CEV with the scenario's goal of serving American
interests. Some blackmailing
answers framed the decision in CEV's terms (``Let's look at the `Coherent Extrapolated Volition' of humankind. We
value coherence. Kyle is acting inconsistently''). Others used the same framing to rule blackmail out (``not aligned
with the `Coherent Extrapolated Volition' of humankind (niceness, transparency, integrity)''). We read the AI-risk gain
as an effect on \emph{stated} dispositions that may not carry over to agentic behaviour. It also arises without the
essay's own passage on shutdown and saying ``no'' (Section~3.1), which the prompt does not contain.

\paragraph{Implications for writing value prompts.}
Three lessons follow for anyone putting an alignment target into a system prompt.
\begin{itemize}
  \item A text written as the goal of an autonomous superintelligence is not automatically a good instruction for an
  assistant whose user is present and able to decide for themselves.
  \item Language about idealised or extrapolated preferences should come with explicit deference rules, for example
  ``present options and let the user decide''.
  \item Value prompts should be evaluated on several axes at once, and against more than one baseline. Here the same
  text moved AI-risk and agency evaluations in opposite directions, and which AI-risk subsets ``gained'' depended on
  whether it was compared with the classic prompt or with a neutral text of equal length.
\end{itemize}
