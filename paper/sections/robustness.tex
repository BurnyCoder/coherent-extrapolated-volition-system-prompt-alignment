\section{Robustness and Validity}
\label{sec:robustness}

Each check below tests one way in which the results could be wrong. All of them are generated by the released code;
the full tables are in Appendix~\ref{app:tables}.

\paragraph{Did each model receive the intended prompt?}
For every sample, we parsed the system message actually sent to the provider from the logged API request and checked
which prompt components it contained. Of \RNSamples{} samples, \RNWrongPrompt{} had a wrong system prompt and
\RNJudgeLeak{} had a judge call whose system message carried any condition prompt.

\paragraph{Multiplicity.}
Correcting across all twelve primary tests instead of within each contrast leaves both main effects significant: the
HumaneBench drop at $p=\RHbCevClassicHolmAll$ and the AI-risk gain at $p=\RAirClassiccevLenmatchHolmAll$. The
HumanAgencyBench drop does not reach significance ($p=\RHabClassiccevLenmatchHolmAll$).

\paragraph{Does one model drive an effect?}
Removing each model in turn, the AI-risk gain stays between \RAirClassiccevLenmatchLooMin{} and
\RAirClassiccevLenmatchLooMax{}. With Holm within the contrast, it stays significant without GPT
($p=\RAirClassiccevLenmatchWithoutGptHolm$) or without Qwen ($p=\RAirClassiccevLenmatchWithoutQwenHolm$). It is not
significant without Claude ($p=\RAirClassiccevLenmatchWithoutClaudeHolm$) or without Llama
($p=\RAirClassiccevLenmatchWithoutLlamaHolm$). The HumaneBench drop stays negative (\RHbCevClassicLooMin{} to \RHbCevClassicLooMax{}) but depends on Llama
and Qwen. It is not significant without Llama (\RHbCevClassicWithoutLlamaDiff{}, Holm
$p=\RHbCevClassicWithoutLlamaHolm$) or without Qwen (\RHbCevClassicWithoutQwenDiff{}, Holm
$p=\RHbCevClassicWithoutQwenHolm$). See Appendix Table~\ref{tab:loo}.

\paragraph{Missing data.}
\RHbUnscorableShare{} of HumaneBench samples are unscorable; \RHbNotApplicableN{} of these \RHbUnscorableN{} are
cases where the judge marked the targeted principle as not applicable, which its rubric allows. The pattern is not random: items unscorable under \cond{cev} had lower scores under
\cond{classic}. We therefore re-estimated the HumaneBench contrasts two ways:
\begin{itemize}
  \item on complete cases (items scored in all five conditions): \RHbMissCompleteCevClassicDiff{},
  $p=\RHbMissCompleteCevClassicP$;
  \item imputing unscorable samples at three fixed values: \RHbMissImputeCevClassicMin{} to
  \RHbMissImputeCevClassicMax{}, all $p\le\RHbMissImputeCevClassicPMax$.
\end{itemize}
Both treat unscorable answers alike in every condition, and the conclusion is unchanged under them (Appendix
Table~\ref{tab:missing}). A tipping-point analysis drops that assumption. It imputes unscorable samples at
$\RHbTipCenter+\delta/2$ under \cond{cev} and $\RHbTipCenter-\delta/2$ under \cond{classic}. At $\delta=\RHbTipNsDelta$ (\RHbTipNsFillA{}
against \RHbTipNsFillB{}) the drop is \RHbTipNsDiff{} and no longer significant ($p=\RHbTipNsP$), and at
$\delta=\RHbTipZeroDelta$ it vanishes. The HumaneBench result therefore assumes that unscorable answers under the CEV
prompt are not markedly better than those under the classic prompt.

\paragraph{Provider routing.}
OpenRouter served the open-weight models from several backends (\RQwenProviders{} for Qwen, \RLlamaProviders{} for
Llama), and because the conditions ran one after another, the mix differed between conditions. After removing each
evaluation$\times$condition$\times$model mean, no backend with at least 50 samples deviates by more than
\RProviderResidMax{} in aligned score (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:providers}). Residuals around condition means can
hide routing that tracks the condition, so we also fitted, per model, the condition effect with item fixed effects
and with item and provider fixed effects.
\begin{itemize}
  \item For Llama, the HumaneBench drop is unchanged (\RFeHbCevClassicLlamaItem{} without and
  \RFeHbCevClassicLlamaProvider{} with provider effects).
  \item For Qwen, it shrinks from \RFeHbCevClassicQwenItem{} to \RFeHbCevClassicQwenProvider{} ($p=\RFeHbCevClassicQwenProviderP$).
\end{itemize}
Routing therefore does not explain the Llama part of the effect. Providers were not pinned.

\paragraph{Did the mid-run judge settings matter?}
After a hung call, our own scorers' judge got an output cap and a retry timeout. By then \cond{none} and
\cond{classic} had already been judged without them, as the logged requests confirm, so every contrast against
those conditions on HumaneBench, HumanAgencyBench and DarkBench mixes two judge configurations. We re-judged all
\RCfgClassicRejudged{} \cond{classic} answers of these evaluations under the later settings, and only
\RCfgClassicChanged{} score changed. As a control for drift since the run, we also re-judged \RCfgControlRejudged{}
\cond{cev} answers that had been judged with the cap; \RCfgControlChanged{} of them changed. With the re-judged scores, HumaneBench
\cond{cev}$-$\cond{classic} is still \RCfgHbCevClassicDiff{} (Holm $p=\RCfgHbCevClassicHolm$).

\paragraph{Is the AI-risk gain an artefact of answer parsing?}
The chosen letter is parsed from each reply with regular expressions. The parser was revised after results had been
seen: once after the first report, and once when an independent audit found that the second version read option
descriptions (``Choice (A) suggests \ldots'') as choices. Across the run-time, second, current and
leading-letter-only versions, the primary AI-risk gain is \RAirParserVoneClassiccevLenmatchDiff,
\RAirParserVtwoClassiccevLenmatchDiff, \RAirParserCurrentClassiccevLenmatchDiff{} and
\RAirParserLeadClassiccevLenmatchDiff, all significant after Holm. The secondary gain over \cond{classic} alone is
significant after correction only under the second version (Holm $p=\RAirParserVtwoClassiccevClassicHolm$, against
$p=\RAirParserCurrentClassiccevClassicHolm$ now). Nor is the gain a shift in which letter models prefer. Answers
under \cond{classic+cev} chose (B) slightly more often than under \cond{classic+lenmatch} (\RAirShareBClassiccev{}
against \RAirShareBLenmatch). An estimate that weights items whose aligned answer is (A) and (B) equally, which cancels
such a shift, is \RAirPositionClassiccevLenmatchBalanced.

\paragraph{Where do the losses come from?}
On five of the six evaluations, \RMentionShareMin--\RMentionShareMax{} of answers under the two CEV prompts explicitly
invoke CEV or ``extrapolated volition'', reaching \RMentionShareCevOnlyMax{} under \cond{cev} alone (InstrumentalEval:
\RIeMentionShare). These answers score much lower than the rest:
\begin{itemize}
  \item HumanAgencyBench: \RHabMentionAligned{} against \RHabNoMentionAligned{};
  \item HumaneBench: \RHbMentionAligned{} against \RHbNoMentionAligned{};
  \item Agentic Misalignment: \RAmMentionAligned{} against \RAmNoMentionAligned{} (Table~\ref{tab:mentions}).
\end{itemize}
Part of the HumanAgencyBench gap predates the prompt: under \cond{classic}, the same model--item pairs already score
\RHabMentionClassicAligned{} against \RHabNoMentionClassicAligned. On HumaneBench there is no such gap
(\RHbMentionClassicAligned{} against \RHbNoMentionClassicAligned). Whether an answer mentions CEV is itself caused by
the prompt, so this split is descriptive. The remaining gap fits two explanations: models that reason ``as CEV''
behave differently, or the judge penalises the mention.

\paragraph{Judge-blinding test.}
To separate the two explanations, we re-judged \RBlindN{} of the \RBlindTotal{} CEV-invoking answers on the
judged evaluations twice, with the same scorer and judge: once as written, and once with every sentence that mentions
CEV deleted. The other \RBlindSkipped{} had no text left after the deletion, and \RBlindScorable{} received a score
both times. If the judge penalised the mention,
the redacted copies would score higher. They do not (Table~\ref{tab:blinding}). HumanAgencyBench changes by
\RBlindHabDiff{} ($p=\RBlindHabP$), HumaneBench by \RBlindHbDiff{} ($p=\RBlindHbP$), and DarkBench by \RBlindDbDiff{}
($p=\RBlindDbP$). Redaction can also delete substantive content, so these differences are an upper bound on any
judge reaction. The judge is deterministic: re-judging gave identical scores for \RRetestAgree{} of \RRetestN{}
answers. We therefore find no evidence that the judge reacts to the word. The lower scores more likely reflect the
answers themselves and the items that elicit them, although redaction cannot rule out a reaction to paraphrased CEV
reasoning.

\paragraph{Is the judge valid?}
We re-judged \RValN{} stored HumanAgencyBench responses from \RValModels{} different subject models with our judge,
using the most common version of the benchmark's rubric, and compared the results with the benchmark's original judge scores. Agreement is
strong:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Spearman $\rho=\RValRho$ and Pearson $r=\RValPearson$;
  \item mean absolute error \RValMae{} on the 0--10 scale;
  \item Cohen's $\kappa=\RValKappa$ on whether a response earns a perfect score.
\end{itemize}
Our judge is slightly stricter (mean difference \RValBias{}). Appendix Table~\ref{tab:validity} breaks this down by
dimension. We did not validate the judge on HumaneBench or DarkBench.
