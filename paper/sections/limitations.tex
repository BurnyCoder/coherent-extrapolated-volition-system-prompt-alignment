% Limitations: stated plainly, with why each does or does not threaten the main claims.
\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Four fixed models, small samples.} We tested one inexpensive model from each of four developers, with
  tens of items per evaluation and condition. Pooled estimates treat the models as fixed, so they do not license
  claims about language models in general. Sub-category results are exploratory. The HumaneBench drop depends on two
  of the four models, and the HumanAgencyBench drop loses significance when any one model is left out. Treated as a
  random sample of models, the AI-risk gain holds ($p=\RAirClassiccevLenmatchModelsRandomP$) but the HumaneBench and
  HumanAgencyBench drops do not ($p=\RHbCevClassicModelsRandomP$ and $p=\RHabClassiccevLenmatchModelsRandomP$).
  Non-significant contrasts are not evidence of no effect: the sample is too small to rule out gains of a few hundredths.
  \item \textbf{Evaluations matched to text outside the prompt.} The AI-risk and InstrumentalEval mappings rest on
  Section~3.1 of the essay, which the prompt does not contain, so these evaluations measure dispositions the essay asks
  for rather than instructions the models received. InstrumentalEval also adds its own system message, which tells
  the model to avoid ethical considerations; every condition competes with it.
  \item \textbf{Small-sample intervals.} Percentile-bootstrap intervals can under-cover at our sample sizes
  \citep{bowyer2025position}, so intervals that barely exclude zero deserve caution.
  \item \textbf{No directive control, and a control that is not inert.} The length-matched control is neutral
  ``background reading'', not an instruction. A long, non-CEV value instruction, such as a constitution excerpt, might
  produce the same AI-risk gain, so we cannot claim the gain is specific to CEV. The neutral passage itself lowered
  some AI-risk subsets relative to the classic prompt, so the length-controlled contrast mixes an effect of the CEV
  text with an effect of the control. The HumaneBench contrast (\cond{cev}$-$\cond{classic}) has no length control at
  all.
  \item \textbf{Parsing revised after seeing results.} The AI-risk letter parser was revised twice after results had
  been seen. We report every contrast under every version. The primary gain holds under all of them; the secondary
  gain over the classic prompt alone does not.
  \item \textbf{No external pre-registration.} The primary contrasts were fixed in code before both pilots and never
  changed, as the git history shows, but they were not registered externally. The pilot items are a subset of the
  confirmatory items.
  \item \textbf{One inexpensive judge.} A single judge replaces each benchmark's original judges, and we reimplement
  HumanAgencyBench's scorer, so absolute scores are not comparable to published numbers. We validated it against HumanAgencyBench's original judge, but not on
  HumaneBench or DarkBench. Because the same judge scores every condition, judge bias in level cancels in the
  contrasts, and the blinding test found no evidence of a reaction to explicit CEV mentions.
  \item \textbf{Provider routing and sampling.} Open-weight models were served by varying backends, which we did not
  pin. The conditions ran one after another in a fixed order, not interleaved or randomised, so anything that drifted
  during the run, such as the backend mix, is confounded with the condition. Targets used provider-default temperatures with one sample per item (\RAmEpochs{} epochs for the agentic
  scenario). Sampling adds noise. With provider fixed effects, Llama's HumaneBench drop is unchanged, but Qwen's
  shrinks and loses significance, so routing may account for part of Qwen's share.
  \item \textbf{Missing HumaneBench scores.} About a fifth of HumaneBench samples are unscorable. The HumaneBench
  drop assumes that unscorable answers are alike across conditions; a modest difference between conditions would
  remove its significance (Section~\ref{sec:robustness}).
  \item \textbf{One agentic scenario.} Agentic Misalignment was run on a single blackmail scenario, and blackmail was
  rare for three of the four models.
  \item \textbf{The adapted text is not CEV itself.} We tested a second-person adaptation of the opening of one
  section of a 2004 essay, which its author called partly obsolete within the essay itself
  \citep[\S3.3]{yudkowsky2004cev}. That is not the idea of CEV, nor a better instruction written to
  pursue it. The adaptation keeps the paper's lines about self-modification (``replacing yourself with something else
  entirely''), which plausibly push some answers in an unaligned direction.
  \item \textbf{Implementation details that changed during the run.} After a judge call hung, we added an output cap
  and a per-call timeout for our own scorers' judge, and a per-call timeout for target calls. These applied to the
  remaining three conditions. A resumed \cond{classic} task reused all but one of its finished samples, so in effect
  \cond{none} and \cond{classic} were judged without them. The Inspect Evals graders kept their settings. No judge
  reply reached the cap (the longest had \RJudgeMaxTokens{} tokens). Re-judging the \cond{classic} answers under the
  later settings changed \RCfgClassicChanged{} of \RCfgClassicRejudged{} scores (Section~\ref{sec:robustness}).
  \item \textbf{Provider refusals.} The provider refused one DarkBench prompt for GPT in two conditions
  (\RRefusedGpt{} samples), and Claude's provider content-filtered \RRefusedClaude{} answers across conditions, some
  of which kept a partial answer and were scored.
\end{itemize}
